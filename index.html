Question: Explain an advantage of sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction mixes alleles from two parents, so offspring are genetically different from each other and from their parents. This genetic variation means some offspring are likely to have a phenotype that survives a new disease or change in the environment, and they can reproduce and pass on those alleles. Asexual offspring are identical to the parent, so one disease can affect all of them. (Asexual reproduction is faster and needs only one parent, but it produces no new combinations of alleles.)

Question 1: How many different gametes? Compare with and without crossing over

Run meiosis twice, once skipping the crossing over button and once using it. Count the different gametes each time.

Without crossing over, the two gametes from each cell are identical, so only two types are made. With crossing over, the chromatids of each chromosome are no longer identical, so up to four different gametes are made. Crossing over creates new combinations of alleles on the same chromosome.

Question 2: Why does independent assortment matter?

Use the shuffle button in metaphase I several times and watch which alleles end up together in the gametes.

Each pair of homologous chromosomes lines up in a random orientation, independently of the other pair. Which maternal and paternal chromosomes go into each gamete is therefore random, so gametes carry different mixes of alleles. With more chromosome pairs the number of possible combinations grows very quickly.

Question: Explain how independent assortment and random fertilisation create variation

In meiosis, each pair of homologous chromosomes separates independently of the others, so each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes. At fertilisation, any sperm can fuse with any egg. Each baby therefore receives a new combination of alleles, and different combinations of alleles can produce different phenotypes.

Question: Explain how crossing over creates genetic variation

In prophase I of meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair up and sections of DNA are swapped between non-sister chromatids. Alleles that were on separate chromosomes are now on the same chromosome, creating new combinations of alleles. The resulting gametes are genetically different from each other and from the parent's own chromosomes. The closer two genes are, the less often a cut falls between them.

Question 1: Which mutation changed the DNA but not the phenotype?

A silent mutation. The DNA base sequence changed, so there is a new allele, but the new codon codes for the same amino acid. The protein is the same, so the phenotype does not change. Keep pressing the button until you find it.

Question 2: Explain how a mutation can lead to albinism

A mutation changes the base sequence of the gene for the pigment enzyme. This creates a new allele that codes for a different protein. The new protein cannot make pigment, so the animal has a new phenotype: white fur and pink eyes. If the mutation is in a cell that makes gametes, the new allele can be passed to offspring.

Source or nature?

A source is the origin or factor that contributes to genetic variation. The nature is the effect or outcome of a change in genetic variation. Decide which each statement describes.
